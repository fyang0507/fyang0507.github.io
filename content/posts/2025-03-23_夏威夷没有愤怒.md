**1.0**

The USS Missouri, which missed the entire attack on Pearl Harbor, now lies at anchor in the harbor’s still waters. A little over a hundred meters off her bow rests the wreck of the Arizona, the fellow battleship that suffered the attack’s most devastating loss of life.

Every fifteen minutes, a guide leads another group of visitors aboard to walk the deck. We look up at the enormous gun barrels aimed into the sky, down at the deck boards once crossed by the Japanese surrender delegation, and over at a placard about a burial at sea.

—On April 11, 1945, during the Battle of Okinawa, Captain William M. Callaghan ordered his crew to bury a kamikaze pilot with full military honors. The pilot, widely believed to have been Petty Officer Second Class Setsuo Ishino, thus received the only known U.S. military funeral for a kamikaze pilot during World War II. Some say it was an act of humanity, respect, and honor between soldiers. I know another answer.

William Callaghan missed the entire attack on Pearl Harbor. William Callaghan had no anger.



After the air raid, the Pacific Fleet jumped at shadows: so many of these brown-skinned residents had Asian faces, and as many as 150,000 bore the suspicious taint of Japanese blood! Then came raids, arrests, internment camps, and sweeping martial law. If you looked Japanese, you were a “Jap.”

Hawaiʻi was only an American territory then, not a state—that would have to wait until 1959—but this did nothing to stop curfews and discrimination from spreading. These residents watched their towns become military bases, then found themselves bombed by Japan for reasons they could not understand; some were ultimately locked away in internment camps. How unfair. But what could they say? This was, after all, a war in which justice had to triumph over evil! As for why evil had appeared out of nowhere in the first place…

The residents of Hawaiʻi submitted to American rule. The residents of Hawaiʻi had no anger.



On March 9, 2025, Waikīkī Beach was livelier than usual. The 29th Honolulu Festival was in full swing. I just could not tell whether this was, as its organizers claimed, a festival celebrating the cultures of Hawaiʻi and the Pacific Rim,[1] or simply a Japanese matsuri. I saw banners in English and Japanese, young girls dancing lightly to Japanese pop, mikoshi and parade floats, and, to close the festival, the Nagaoka Fireworks with their “appeal for peace.”

(Curiously, the Nagaoka Fireworks grew out of a festival begun in Nagaoka, Niigata, the year after Japan surrendered, in memory of those who died in World War II.[2] And Honolulu and Nagaoka just happen to be sister cities.)

Many people have much to say about advancing peace. But the perpetrator should never be the one preaching it to the victim, the invader to the invaded, the colonizer to the colonized. I wonder: the world’s most famous beach is only a twenty-minute drive from the most humiliating wound in American military history, and plenty of tourists shuttle between the two in a single day—what kind of harmony is this? How do people equally fluent in Japanese and English understand who they are?

I know someone will say that sometimes it is wiser not to look too closely. Let the past be past.

Indeed.

In any case, Hawaiʻi has no anger.



**2.0**

When Captain James Cook reached Hawaiʻi, the story goes, it was Makahiki, the season when Native Hawaiians celebrated Lono, the akua of agriculture, harvest, and peace. That coincidental login window led them to receive Captain Cook as a god.

They were less pleased when the captain appeared a second time. The festival was over; so were its rites and obligations. Why had he come back? And so Cook fell into the mortal world and was killed by Hawaiian warriors.

Cook was not Lono. But he came with a god’s power and a god’s punishment all the same. The epidemics that followed in his footsteps would kill nine-tenths of the Hawaiian people over the next century; the missionaries who came in his wake would raise the Christian cross at this crossroads of the Pacific.

Hawaiʻi had never shut itself away from the world. Its chiefs used Western weapons to win their wars of unification; a Hawaiian queen advanced Western faith and culture in place of traditional religion and thought. Yet all that keeping up with the times did little to free them from what had already been ordained. In 1893, plantation owners overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani after she tried to secure greater power for Native Hawaiians, ending nearly a century of monarchical rule over the islands. Five years later, from Washington, D.C., eight thousand kilometers away, came the Newlands Resolution, declaring on behalf of everyone in Hawaiʻi: Hawaiʻi was American territory.

Then came mass immigration: Japan, China, Portugal, Korea, the Philippines… In the decades that followed, Hawaiʻi’s population more than doubled. Half a century later, it was the descendants of these immigrants who pushed America to admit Hawaiʻi as the fiftieth state. This annexed Pacific kingdom had finally found a place inside the Constitution.

By then, it was already difficult to say who counted as a “local” in Hawaiʻi.



**3.0**

According to an old legend, taking a rock from Hawaiʻi without permission will bring bad luck—unless, of course, you buy it from a local.

Meanwhile, tourists hike wherever they please on Haleakalā and watch the sun rise and set, though in Hawaiian culture the sacred summit was climbed for ceremony, not casual sightseeing.



From the fences drawn across them and the warning signs posted nearby, certain ponds and beaches are not to be approached lightly. Only locals may enter the water.

Meanwhile, tourists drink contentedly at a lūʻau and watch locals onstage perform exaggerated Polynesian war cries and grass-skirt dances.



According to the guidebooks, drivers on the Road to Hāna should watch for local traffic and promptly pull over to let it pass.

Meanwhile, the 2023 fire burned most of Lāhainā and destroyed local homes. Rebuilding has been slow, while the neighboring resorts remain packed and clamorous.



The flag inherited from the Hawaiian Kingdom snaps and struts all over these islands of the United States. Meanwhile, 160,000 new American tourists arrive every day, pulling out their sunglasses and aloha shirts as they land.

Who needs this kind of condescending respect? Who truly owns Hawaiʻi?



**4.0**

In the forest beyond Hāna, bamboo said to have been brought by Chinese laborers has shot up into a lush, riotous grove. In Lāhainā, the Indian banyan planted to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the first Protestant mission there still stands, casting its broad shade. At the windswept summit of a volcanic park, chukar partridges introduced from Asia peck at the gaps between parked cars. These introduced species live contentedly here. Sometimes they grow far too brazen: once guests, they take over as masters.

But capitalism is the true master here.

Thirty-seven American billionaires own private holdings amounting to more than five percent of all the land in the state.[3] Larry Ellison alone—the co-founder of Oracle—owns 98 percent of the land on Lānaʻi. (At sunset, seen from Maui’s western shore, Lānaʻi’s immense shadow looks like a mourning shroud laid across Lāhainā. What did Larry do after the fire? From what I can find in the public record, nothing.)

A century earlier, the five plantation conglomerates known as the Big Five held more than ten percent of all the land in Hawaiʻi between them. This was when the face of Hawaiʻi changed at breakneck speed: Native species and forest landscapes disappeared, replaced by sugarcane fields stretching to the horizon, pineapple plantations, cattle beyond counting… Only with the rise of tourism did people suddenly remember fragile ecosystems, rare species, and environmental conservation. Then golf courses arrived late, posing as the final victor, only to spread across hill after hill. Look down on Maui from an airplane today and the plains, half meadow and half desert, resemble wounds that stubbornly refuse to heal.

It is hard to call Hawaiʻi ravaged: life does not fail such abundant rain and sunlight.

Whatever the goddess Pele cannot save, Uncle Sam ravages all the more.



**5.0**

In one corner of the Sunday market beside the Honolulu Zoo, two men who looked like brothers had set up a canopy to sell handicrafts. Both were dark from the sun, with Asian features, and seemed to be in their fifties. The younger one sat on a makeshift chair polishing a wood carving. The older one, in flip-flops and a camouflage baseball—, waited on customers.

But there were no customers. At eight in the morning, the tourists’ snores had not yet risen into a din. Many of the vendors were still laying out their tables. Only thin morning light dripped through the gaps in the leaves.

The carvings they sold were not the interchangeable tiki figures so common in souvenir shops. Instead, they were all sorts of ornaments, unmistakably the handwork of a self-taught craftsman. One hanging decoration of flying pigs caught our attention. The three little wooden pigs were roughly carved and left entirely unpainted. Strung together and hung from the frame of the canopy, they looked like a wind chime. Behind each pig was a three-lobed cluster of bird feathers.

March is a windy month in Hawaiʻi. When the wind rose, the feathers began to turn. There was no bell anywhere on the ornament, and yet I could suddenly hear the clear, tinkling note of a wind chime.

We bought the crude little pigs. When the seller rose on tiptoe to take them down, I noticed that his eyes crossed slightly, making him look all the more guileless. As I write these words, the pigs are spinning before our window. March in New York is windy, too.

Whenever I pass them, I cannot help stopping to blow at them. As I watch their feathers rustle and turn, I feel a little stronger and a little moved.

I suppose I have said many bad things about Hawaiʻi. I thought that to visit in a vacationer’s frame of mind was to miss the point. But stay too long among these folds of history and double exposures of culture, and sooner or later you begin to swallow water and lose heat.

And perhaps Hawaiʻi has no anger for another reason: no matter how heavily the days wear on, there remains a way to live, small but still warm and strong. These ordinary things are what hold us up. They hold the Earth steady in its turning, too.

詹姆斯·库克船长到达夏威夷时正值玛卡希基节（Makahiki），这正是当地土著庆祝洛诺（Lono）——农业、丰收与和平之神的时候。这巧合的登录节点让夏威夷人将库克船长奉为神明。

船长第二次出现时他们却不高兴了。节日和侍奉已经结束，他又为什么要回来？于是库克堕入凡间，被夏威夷战士杀死。

库克确实不是洛诺，但他一样拥有神的力量和神的惩罚：那些跟随他的脚步上岸的瘟疫和传教士们，在之后的一个世纪杀死了九成的夏威夷人，又在这个太平洋的十字路口升腾起基督的十字。

夏威夷人历史上没有闭关锁国：夏威夷的酋长们借助西方的武器赢的部落战争，夏威夷的女王推行西方的信仰和文化替代传统的宗教和观念，但这些与时俱进并没有让他们逃逸出更多的命中注定。1893年，试图为土著争取更多权力的女王被种植园主推翻，了结了这些岛屿近百年的君主统治；五年后，源自华盛顿特区的纽兰兹决议替所有8000公里外的夏威夷人做出声明：夏威夷是美国的领地。

然后它迎来了大规模的移民：日本，中国，葡萄牙，韩国，菲律宾……十余年间，夏威夷的人口暴涨一倍。而半个世纪后，正是这些移民的后代推动美国承认它成为美利坚合众国的第五十州。这个被吞并的太平洋王国，终于在宪法之内找到了位置。

只是这时候，你已经和难说清楚，谁才是夏威夷的“本地人”了。

与此同时，游客在哈莱阿卡拉火山（Haleakalā）上任意徒步和观看日出日落，尽管在真正的夏威夷文化中，登上圣山的唯一理由是祭祀神灵。



根据一些拉上的围栏和竖起的警示，有些池塘或海滩不可贸然光顾，只有本地人可以下水游泳。

夏威夷王国的旗帜在合众国的岛屿上到处耀武扬威地猎猎作响。与此同时，每天都有16万新的美国游客拿出自己的墨镜和夏威夷衬衫登陆旅行。

**4.0**

哈纳的原始森林里，中国劳工带来的竹苗闹腾得蹿成了一片郁郁的竹林。为纪念清教徒登陆50周年而种下的印度榕树屹立不倒荫庇一方。火山公园多风的山顶，自亚洲引进的石鸡在停车场的间隙低头觅食。这些外来物种在这里生活得怡然自得，有时过分嚣张，反客为主。

但资本主义才是这里真正的主人。

[37个美国富豪拥有的私人领地超过全州土地面积的5%](https://www.forbeschina.com/city/67247)，其中仅拉里·埃里森（甲骨文创始人）一人就拥有98%的拉奈岛。（夕照时分，从茂宜的西岸望去，拉奈岛巨大的阴影像给拉海纳披上一件丧衣。那场大火过后拉里做了什么呢？从公开资料看，他什么都没有做。）

而一个世纪前，五大种植业的卡特尔的土地面积合计[超过总体的10%](https://chatgpt.com/share/67dee245-3504-8012-81d5-59cf1f10e879)。那是夏威夷风貌急剧变化的时期：本地物种和森林景观的消失，取而代之以一望无际的甘蔗田、菠萝农场、数不胜数的黄牛……直到旅游业的兴起，人们这才想起生态脆弱、珍稀物种、环境保护。于是高尔夫球场以最后的胜利者的姿势姗姗来迟，却开得漫山遍野。如今从飞机上俯瞰茂宜岛，那些半是草甸半是荒漠的平原像是固执的不肯愈合的创伤。

很难用疮痍来形容夏威夷，因为生命不会辜负那些丰沛的雨水和阳光。

只是，但凡佩蕾女神拯救不了的东西，山姆大叔就会变本加厉。



**5.0**

檀香山动物园旁边的周日集市的一个角落，看上去像兄弟的两人支着一个蓬子在售卖手工艺品。他们被日光晒得黝黑，亚洲面孔，似乎都是五十岁上下。弟弟坐在一把简陋的椅子上正在给一个木雕抛光，哥哥穿着拖鞋戴一顶迷彩的棒球招呼客人。

但并没有什么客人。清晨八点，游人的呼噜还没有变成喧嚣，集市里不少卖家还在布置自己的台面，只是晨光稀稀疏疏地从叶缝间滴落。

他们卖的木雕并不是纪念品店里面常常陈列的大同小异的萨满像，而一看便知是自学成才的匠人手工的各式装饰。我们被一个飞天小猪的吊饰吸引住了。这三只木头小猪被雕得很简陋，没有任何涂色，它们被线串成一串，像风铃一样被挂在蓬子的支架上。每只小猪的屁股后面都插着三叶鸟羽。

三月的夏威夷本是多风的时候。风起时，这些羽毛迎风转起来。吊饰上没有任何铃铛，而我却能恍然听见清脆的风铃声。

我们买下了这些粗糙的小猪。老板踮着脚去摘下它的时候，我才看出他有些对眼，因此也显出憨厚的样子。就在我写下这些文字的时候，这些小猪正在我们的窗前滴溜溜地转着，三月的纽约也是多风的。

我每次从小猪前面走过的时候，总忍不住停下来对着它们吹气，看着它们的羽毛簌簌转动，我都能获得一些力量和一些感动。

我想，我说了好多夏威夷的坏话。我以为以度假的心态去夏威夷是不得要领的。但呆在这些历史褶皱和文化叠影中太久，人总会呛水和失温。

夏威夷没有愤怒，也可以是因为就算日子再过沉重，仍然有一种渺小却不失温暖和力量的活法，正是这些平凡的东西支撑着我们，也支撑着地球平稳转动。
