More than a century ago, Romain Rolland opened *Beethoven*, the first of his *Lives of Illustrious Men*, with these words:

> “The air is heavy around us. Old Europe lies numb in a heavy, tainted atmosphere. A base materialism, devoid of grandeur, weighs on thought and obstructs the action of governments and individuals. The world is dying of suffocation in its cautious and abject egoism.” [1]

Over the next half-century, Europe endured the devastation of two world wars, then became the front line of a bipolar world, swept along by a current—and an overt grand design—more ambitious even than the Marshall Plan... Europe was not brought to its knees, but neither did it ever fully recover the influence it had wielded at its height.

More recently, I have watched it hem and haw and change the subject after the Nord Stream pipeline explosions. I have watched it make moves in two regional conflicts that do not entirely serve its own interests... Europe has lost more than influence. It is surrendering its dignity.

I do not say this with regret. The heroes in *Lives of Illustrious Men* exhilarated me, but traveling through Europe again nine years later, I also watched with great interest as the anguish that drove Romain Rolland to write failed to make the continent any more clearheaded.



I first heard of Ghent on social media: in the Middle Ages, it was Europe’s second-largest city after Paris. [2] Walking through it, I was astonished by how completely its historic architecture had been preserved, and by the sheer scale of it all. Its accumulated history spills from the glass like wine. You can smell the years in every statue along its streets, every paving stone, every bell. Pointing to a tavern—Café Den Turk—a local guide told me that its owner had once joined the Crusades.

Ghent once teemed with people, and many of its buildings were erected by guilds of boatmen, tanners, tax collectors, textile workers... This rising bourgeoisie flourished in a commercial powerhouse that had once towered over Europe. Then came the Revolt of Ghent.



The revolt began when the citizens of Ghent rose up against Charles V—Holy Roman Emperor and himself a native of Ghent—over new taxes imposed to expand his wars. To put down the revolt, Charles V spent nearly half a year making his way from Spain to Ghent, gathering five thousand soldiers along the way.

The people of Ghent did not resist... Charles V made his triumphant entrance, settled into his own palace, and set about dealing with the rebels.

In the end, the people of Ghent were ordered to dress in robes and walk barefoot, nooses around their necks, from the Town Hall to Charles’s palace to beg forgiveness. [3]

Ghent did not forget. To make sure it never would, the city even named a locally brewed beer after the episode. Its residents call themselves *Stroppendragers*—noose-bearers—and every year, during the local festivities, they reenact the scene in celebration of their ancestors’ spirit of resistance.



By the time the guide finished, I felt as though I no longer recognized the word resistance. When I think of resistance, I think of martyrs, I think of ideals, I think of heroes. I never think of beer...

Half out of curiosity and half to put my hunch to the test, I asked the guide why, despite World War II, Ghent’s buildings remained so intact.

Because Ghent was not strategically important, he said. It was used as a German logistics base. Between occupation and liberation, no serious armed conflict took place there.

That settled it for me. *People’s war* does, after all, have its applicable domains.



The handsome gateway of Ghent’s Old Fish Market bears a male figure on one side and a female figure on the other, representing the Scheldt and the Lys, the two rivers that flow through the city. [4] The man, I was told, had been modeled on a local playboy at the time of its construction.

If your ancestors lived with this kind of irreverence, you, too, would find it hard to take many things too seriously.



Ilya Ehrenburg wrote in his memoirs: “In 1950, Europe lived a poor, anxious, hectic life. The workers’ battle in France and Italy had already been lost in 1947, but everyone felt the battle was still going on.” [5]

Europeans do not know how to make revolution. Even when they do, it is a leisurely revolution. Europeans are too good at living. They start drinking at breakfast. Even the little convenience stores in their train stations carry two or three kinds of buttermilk and bread baked on the premises. Walk down an alley and the rooms along the street are warmly furnished, their dining tables often crowded with brilliant flowers... For Europeans, so many things matter just a little more than the future.

The guide pointed to a building painted yellow. It was the only wooden building left in Ghent’s old city, he said. A local legend had it that the lady of the house once swore to her husband, as he set off to war, that she would not change her clothes until he returned. He was gone so long that the house slowly took on the color of the underwear worn by this lady, who had remained so steadfastly true to her original vow.

In that indecent joke, I saw Ghent’s long yet lighthearted history. Romain Rolland’s admonition, by contrast, was cast in such heavy words. He was the true anomaly.

一个多世纪以前，罗曼·罗兰在《名人传》开篇写道：

“我们周围的空气多沉重！老大的欧罗巴在重浊与腐败的气氛中昏迷不醒。鄙俗的物质主义镇压着思想，阻挠着政府与个人的行动。社会在乖巧卑下的自私自利中窒息以死。“

自那之后的半个世纪，欧洲历经两次世界大战的浩劫，又在比马歇尔计划更为宏大的趋势和阳谋中，成为世界两极对抗的前线……欧洲并没有一蹶不振，但也确实再没完全回复它最为鼎盛的影响力。

而最近，我看着它在北溪管道事件后的支支吾吾顾左右而言他，看着它在两场区域冲突中做出并不完全符合自身利益的举措……欧洲不仅是失去了影响力，它根本是在放弃自己的尊严。

我并不是带以遗憾的口气说这句话的；我振奋于《名人传》中的英雄故事，但九年之后再次游历欧洲，我也饶有兴致地看着罗曼·罗兰痛切心扉的写作动机并没有让这片大陆变得更加清醒。



我是从社交媒体上才知道根特——中世纪欧洲仅次于巴黎的第二大城市。漫步其中，我惊讶于它对古代建筑保存之完整和其规模本身的宏大。它的累累历史像杯中满溢而出的葡萄酒，你可以从这些街衢的任何一个雕塑，任何一个石板，任何一口吊钟嗅到充满年头的气息：当地的向导指着一个酒馆（Café Den Turk）说，它的老板曾经参加了十字军东征。

根特在历史上人潮聚集，其许多建筑都是当年由公会（Guild）出资修建——船夫的，皮匠的，收税官的，纺织业的……这些新兴资产阶级在这个曾睥睨欧洲的繁华商业重镇曾烜赫一时，然后就发生了“根特起义（Revolt of Ghent）”。



这场起义是根特市民反对查理五世——神圣罗马皇帝，一个根特人——为扩大战争而新增的税负而发起的。查理五世为了镇压起义，用了近半年的从西班牙去往根特，并沿途召集了五千名士兵。

根特人没有抵抗……查理五世堂而皇之地进了城，住进了自己的宫殿，开始着手处理叛乱分子。

事件最后，根特人被命令身穿长袍，赤脚，并颈负绞索，从市政厅游行至国王的宫殿请罪。

根特人没有忘记这件事情，为了不至于忘记，他们还特地以此命名了一种当地酿造的啤酒。根特居民自称“套索人（noose bearers）”，他们会在每年的节日上重现这个节目，庆祝自己祖先的反抗精神。



听完这个故事，我发现自己好像不认识反抗这个词了。提起反抗，我习惯于想起先烈，想起理想，想起英雄，但就是不会想起任何啤酒……

半是出于好奇，半是出于实证自己的猜想，我问那个讲完故事的当地向导说：为什么哪怕历经了第二次世界大战，根特的建筑群却这么完好呢？

他说，因为这里不是什么战略要地，而被用作德军的后勤地。这里从被侵占到得到解放期间，并没有发生什么严重的武装冲突。

我确信了，人民战争这个词毕竟有它的适用范围。



根特的水产市场（Fish Market）的漂亮拱门上左右各有一男一女的形象，代表流经根特的两条河流；而其男性的形象是根据建造时期根特城里一位花花公子的面容雕刻而成的。

如果你的先辈们是以这样戏谑的方式生活的，你肯定也不会较真地去看待很多事情。



伊利亚·爱伦堡在回忆录中写道：“（1950年）欧洲过着贫穷、不安、慌张的生活。在法国和意大利，工人的战斗早在1947年即已失败，但大家都觉得战斗仍在继续。”

欧洲人是不会革命的，就算革命，那也是悠闲的革命。欧洲人太会生活了，他们从早饭就开始喝酒，火车站的不大的便利店里也有两三种酪浆和现场烘焙的面包。打小巷中穿过，那些道旁的房间里陈设温馨，经常还会在饭桌上插满娇艳的鲜花……他们把很多东西都看得比未来更重要一点。

向导指着一幢漆成黄色的建筑说，那是根特老城里唯一的木质建筑了。他说根特居民间流传着这样的传说，说这家的女主人向当兵远征的丈夫发誓说不见他归来她就不换衣物，但丈夫久别未归，于是这栋房子就慢慢变成了这位坚守初心的女士的内裤的颜色。

我在这个不雅的笑话中看到了根特虽然漫长但是轻松的历史。罗曼·罗兰的谆告中用了那么沉重的词，他才是那个异类啊。
