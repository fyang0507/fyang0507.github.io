When I traveled alone through the South of France, the sun seemed reluctant to leave the sky. It was broad daylight before six in the morning, and at nine in the evening the sky was still clear. By afternoon, the sun bore down without the slightest cover, as if it had the whole city by the throat. The discomfort reached through the entire body.

Along the seafront promenade of each city, rows of tall palms were interspersed with movable chairs of dark-blue iron. At first glance, they looked like a municipal amenity thoughtfully provided for tourists who wished to ogle the women in bikinis on the beach. Unexpectedly, plenty of locals enjoyed them too. Young people listened to music; older people read the newspaper, pausing over Sudoku grids and word puzzles.

The decision to make the chairs movable had clearly been considered with care. As the sun crossed the sky, people could shift their chairs and follow the shade.

The South of France is beautiful in sunlight. But to a tourist determined to walk along the sea, direct sun is more than a physical force that tans the skin. It assaults the mind, too, and can sour the easiest of moods. I suspect this is precisely why Singapore has so few uncovered pedestrian walkways. I also suspect that Singapore owes its reputation as the “Garden City” to the absence of this experience: tourists can spend almost their entire visit inside air-conditioned coaches.

On the way to Cannes, sitting in a bus whose air-conditioning was nowhere near strong enough, I kept half-consciously mistaking the cities we passed for Oran, Camus's plague-stricken city. I no longer know which came first in that reflex. Oran may lie on the southern shore of the Mediterranean and Nice and Cannes on the northern one, but all three belong to the same rim.

In Camus's eyes, Oran was “a town without intimations.” Its climate gave it a “feverish yet casual air.”

These days, I place great importance on the fit between a person's nature and the place in which they live. In the tropics, for instance, one simply has to make a habit of siestas: at the hottest point of the day, trying to concentrate on anything is nearly futile. Only after walking beneath palms in a blazing sun do you understand that utter unconcern is the only way to remain at ease. If you still have something to insist on, something you feel compelled to say, those clammy waves of heat will leave you extraordinarily irritable.

That irritation is almost a biological instinct. Yet it is not an impulse that sends you charging toward shade in one determined burst. Rather, it is an irrational, irresponsible willingness to go on exposing yourself to discomfort. You could almost say that a person in such a state is on the edge of degradation. How can I put it? In that moment, you think: it would make no difference if the rest of my life went on exactly like this. I doubt people in the tropics are much afraid of Christianity's eternal hellfire. To be scorched is not to have your attention fixed on the flesh-rending agony of your own body. It dissipates the mind instead, leaving it blank, idle, and indifferent to whatever may happen.

Freezing is the inverse. Cold forces the mind to concentrate. Physically, the pores constrict and the whole body works to conserve heat; mentally, the corresponding state is one of inward compression.

But back to Oran: what did the plague become for those who lived through it? Whether Camus intended it or not, his choice of setting deepens our understanding of the everyday. The plague is terrifying. Yet those who live beneath its shadow long enough lose their first fear and alarm. They learn to inhabit the new normal as though nothing were out of the ordinary. In Camus, the evidence of reality—or our apprehension of it—renders calamity utterly unspectacular: “nothing is less sensational than pestilence,” and “by reason of their very duration great misfortunes are monotonous.” Under the hot, dry conditions of a Mediterranean summer, I believe this blunting of perception would be markedly amplified. That dissipative, *can't-be-bothered* state of mind would greatly hasten the residents' adaptation.

In the end, there may be no advice worth offering. I have only recorded an observable phenomenon and an inward experience of my own. Yet this traffic between body and mind does illuminate the old saying, “A still mind brings its own coolness.” Seen from here, do idealism and materialism still look like opposite extremes?

我在南法独自旅行的时候，东升西落的太阳总是长久地滞留在天空之上。六点不到已经大亮，九点的天色依然晴朗；而尤其是午后的时候，阳光总是毫无遮掩地打下来，整个城市就像被扼住了咽口一般让人浑身难受。

每个城市的海滨大道上除了一株株高大的棕榈，还有许多可以活动的深蓝色铁制椅子。乍一看似乎是为好色的游客可以专心观赏海滩的比基尼美女而被细心安置的政府福利工程，可是令人意外的是，似乎很多当地人也喜欢坐在上面休憩；年轻人听音乐，老年人则会看报纸，顺带着做做报纸上的九宫格和words puzzle.

显然设计成可以移动的椅子是经过认真考量的。随着一天之中太阳位置的变化，人们就可以移动椅子让自己始终处于树荫之下。

阳光下的南法是美丽的，然而对于一定要漫步滨海大道的游客来说，太阳的直射不仅是一种可以晒黑肤色的物理力量，也是一种足以毁掉轻松心情的精神攻击。我相信正式这种感觉，才让新加坡异常地缺乏露天的人行道；也正是缺少了这种体验（游客几乎可以在开着冷气的旅行车里度过），新加坡才有了花园城市的美誉。

当我坐在空调开得非常不足的公交车中向着戛纳行进的时候，我总是有意无意地把途经的城市认做阿赫兰、那座加缪笔下疫病爆发的城市。我当然不知道这种思考反射的先后顺序，总之阿赫兰与尼斯、戛纳，尽管位于南北两岸，却都是地中海沿边的城市。

在加缪的眼中，阿赫兰是一座“毫无臆想”的城市；而在气候的作用下，充满了一种“既狂热，又心不在焉”的神气。

我现在非常强调一个人的天性与其身处环境之间的相性，比如热带的人民是一定要养成午睡的习惯的，因为在一天里最炎热的时刻，人们如果期望能集中精力做些什么事情，那几乎是徒劳的。当你切切实实走在棕榈和烈阳之下，你才知道只有那种蛮不在乎的心境才能让人过得怡然自得。否则，如果人还需要坚持什么东西，热衷于表达什么言语的话，你就会被这种汗涔涔的热浪搅得异常地烦躁。

那种烦躁几乎是一种生物性的本能，那并不是一种急于寻找荫蔽的一鼓作气往前走下去的推动力，而是一种毫无责任心地将自己持续暴露在使人不舒服的环境下的非理性态度。你几乎可以说人在那种状态下是接近堕落的。怎么说呢，在那种时刻人是拥有这样的心态的——余下的人生就这么度过也毫无所谓。我想热带的人民是不会怎么惧怕基督教中地狱的永恒烈火的炙烤的，因为所谓炙烤并不是会让人专注于关注自身肉体撕心裂肺的痛苦，而是驱使人将头脑放空的耗散，对任何情况不以为意的无所事事。

与之相反的是冰冻。从相反的意义上说，寒冷是迫使人集中精力的。即使从肉体上来讲，寒冷状态下的毛孔收缩，全身以贮存热量的形式活动着——这在相对应的精神上就表现为一种向内压缩的状态。

回过头来说，阿赫兰的居民在鼠疫中是一种怎样的体验呢？加缪的这种设定，无论说是否有意，恰巧加深了人们对于日常性的理解。鼠疫是可怕的，然而长期生活下疫情笼罩下的人们将最终失去担惊受怕的最初体验，而尽可以若无其事地面对这种新常态。加缪说，这种“现实的明白无误性”使得灾祸比任何东西都不壮观，而且，“巨大的祸患时间之长本身就十分单调。”而我相信，身处地中海夏季高温少雨的气候条件下，这种感知的钝感将会被相当明显地放大；“不想管”的耗散心态将大大加速居民的适应过程。

最后，作为结论似乎也并没有什么值得提出的建议。我仅仅是记述了一种可供观察到的现象和个人的内心体验。然而，从这种身心相互作用的例子中，我们也能了解“心静自然凉”所蕴含的理趣了；而从这个意义上说，唯心和唯物难道还是思想形态的两种极端么？
