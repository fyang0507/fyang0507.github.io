At Hong Kong immigration, the officer in the booth next to mine asked a young woman, “May I ask the purpose of your visit? How many days will you be staying?”

“Are you pregnant?”

When she said no, the officer gave her a faint smile—the smile of a quality inspector whose product has passed inspection.

Welcome, mainland middle class. Welcome to the Hong Kong forest.



**1.0**

Looking out to port from the expressway bridge linking the airport to Hong Kong Island, I saw the immense booms of container cranes drawn up beside it as if awaiting inspection. Beyond them, gleaming high-rises stood like prisms, drawing out of land and sky a blue-green that had belonged to neither. We can imagine the tender fairy tales behind those little windows, inside those narrow apartments, each one carefully partitioned and wrapped in that same blue-green.

To keep those fairy tales alive, Hong Kong’s middle class must obey the primal command-and-response of Trisolaran civilization:

Do not look beyond. Do not look beyond. Do not look beyond.

Do not look beyond, and you will never see the walls of lightless alleys, blackened by cooking smoke.

Do not look beyond, and you will never look up to find a skyful of ants beating enormous wings in the rainy season.

Do not look beyond, and you will never meet, head-on, the eyes of the stranger sharing your table in a crowded *bing sutt*.

Do not look beyond. However the world may change, never ask what happened across the river.



**2.0**

At dusk over Victoria Harbour, the rooftop signs on most of the skyscrapers had already lit up. The Lee Kum Kee sign remained dark. It was not that someone had forgotten to switch it on; the sign looked as though it had never contained neon in the first place. Yet in this shoulder-to-shoulder forest of towers, even its age did not make it stand out.

Hong Kong’s first skyscraper was not completed until 1973. Then development became unstoppable. Towers kept rising until the land was packed tight and there was no room left for another. Most of the buildings around Victoria Harbour today went up in the two decades spanning the turn of the century.

Across Hong Kong’s modern surfaces, you can always taste something that has been steeping for years, like old master stock bleeding through the lacquered skin of a pig’s trotter. It is a flavor only human beings as close to expiry as the stock itself can understand.

Here we see the sunless side of Masayoshi Son’s “time-machine” theory. When people from a later-rising region visit a place once at the forefront, what strikes them first is not its former prosperity—which belongs to a past they cannot see—but its decline, made visible by comparison with places that now look more modern.

I think the dense ranks of towers around this harbour are a sophon flung by the people of a previous era into the Hong Kong of today. At that moment, everyone was racing to reach both hands toward the sky and the summit of the world. No one imagined that these colossi of concrete and rebar might together form a higher-dimensional object, their vast etching at last unfolding, slowly, in the river of time—until every high-rise is hemmed in by another, until people wake from the myth of creation—

Hong Kong’s skyline is locked fast.

Hong Kong is locked fast.



**3.0**

At noon on Saturday, sheets of Oxford cloth were spread along the railings of a pedestrian footbridge in Mong Kok. Women in headscarves gathered in twos and threes and settled on the ground, their voices gradually rising into a hubbub. They paid no attention to the crowd passing by. They called friends over, poured drinks from economy-size bottles into disposable cups, or opened video-chat apps and carried on loud speakerphone conversations with blurred, shifting figures at the other end.

For a while I could not make sense of what I was seeing. There was none of the tension that precedes a political gathering, and none of the commerce of a market. Still the crowd kept growing. Eventually, a few patrol officers came by. I stopped them and learned that this was how Hong Kong’s domestic helpers usually spent their weekends off.

The next day, in Central, I saw the gathering on a grander scale. No one here wore a headscarf, and a higher-order settlement had evolved: the women built compartment after compartment from cardboard, and some had even pitched tents. One woman brought out a microphone and sang as though no one else existed. Others set up tripods and danced with friends for a Douyin livestream. Still others had opened an eyebrow-shaping business.

And all of it was happening on footbridges.

The footbridges weave crosswise through a forest of towers that knows only how to grow upward; together they form a root system spanning the city. Five tireless days a week, these roots carry nourishment into the CBD. On weekends, they grow a throat. The weekly tide feels like a hand lowering Hong Kong’s photographic negative into developer, again and again: each dip draws out the hidden image beneath the purely functional face of its municipal infrastructure, unearthing the mushrooms that live below the soil of this urban forest.

Ah, mushrooms! While you can, pour yourselves out; tend one another’s mycelia. For once you return behind those little partitions, you will still have to obey the laws of the forest, still tuck one another’s loneliness away, still choose silence for efficiency’s sake.

请问您是否怀孕？

得到否定的回答后，审查员露出一抹微笑（这是质检员面向质检合格的产品的微笑）。

欢迎你，大陆中产阶级。欢迎来到香港森林。

从连结机场和港岛的高速大桥上向左舷望出去，集装箱起吊机巨大的悬臂林立桥畔如同等待检阅。但就在它们身旁，一幢幢辉煌的高楼棱镜般提炼出这片大地和天空中本不存在的青色。我们可以想象，在这些狭窄的公寓里，在那一片片小窗之后，有同样泛着青色的，被精确区隔和包裹的温柔童话。

要想让童话继续下去，香港中产阶级必须恪守三体文明的第一声遵告和回答：

不要眺望。不要眺望。不要眺望。

不要眺望，你就不会窥见不透光的巷道里被油烟熏黑的墙壁。

不要眺望，你就不会抬头看清那雨季中扑腾巨大翅膀的漫天蚁群。

傍晚的维多利亚港，大多数摩天大楼顶部的灯牌都亮起了灯，李锦记的却没有。不是没有点亮，而是它的招牌看上去像是根本没有霓虹装饰。但即使这样，在这摩肩接踵的高楼的森林里，它居然也没有因其陈旧而显得过分招摇。

香港的首栋摩天大楼在1973年才落成，但接下来的开发工程就一发不可收拾，直至那片土地被高楼填满，直到再也放不下更多的高楼。如今维多利亚港矗立的楼群，大部分是在世纪之交那二十年的时间拔地而起的。

香港的现代元素之中你总能咂摸出一些有点年头的东西出来，像是鲜亮的猪蹄之中渗出的陈年老卤，这卤汁也只有和它一样快过期的人类才能理解其中的况味。

我们由此可以发现孙正义“时光机器”理论的背阳面。当后起之秀地区的人们来到曾经的先进之地，他们首先不会感慨它的（不被看见的曾经的）繁华而是感慨它的（通过和更现代的对比而显出的）凋零。

我想，这片港湾里的密集的楼群，正是上一个时代的人投向今天的香港的智子。在那个争先恐后向天空和世界之巅伸出双手的时刻，没有人想到这些用水泥和钢筋浇筑的庞然大物，居然是这样一个高维物体：它们的巨大蚀刻，终于在时间长河里慢慢展开——当每一栋高楼旁边都站满另一栋高楼的时候，当人们从造物的神话中回过神来——

香港的天际线已经被锁死了。

香港已经被锁死了。



**3.0**

周六中午，旺角的人行天桥上，一张张牛津布沿着栏杆铺展开来。戴头巾的妇女三三两两开始聚集，席地而坐，人声逐渐鼎沸。这些妇女并不对行人有任何兴趣；她们只是呼朋引伴，把量贩装的饮料倒进一次性杯子里；或者开启视频聊天程序，和另一头模糊晃动的人影通过扬声器和她们大声对话。

我对当下发生的事情一时摸不着头脑。它看起来没有政治集会前的紧张感，也没有显示出任何集市的商业活动的迹象。但人确实是越来越多。直至旁边走过几个巡警，我拉住他们询问，才知道这是香港的家政服务人员周末的典型休憩活动。

第二天，我在中环看见了更为宏大的聚集。这里没有人戴头巾，并且发展出了更为高阶的聚落：她们用纸板建起了一个个隔间，有的甚至搭起了帐篷。有人拿出话筒，旁若无人地唱起歌来；有人立起三脚架和姐妹在镜头前跳舞做抖音直播；有人搞起了修眉生意。

而这一切都是在天桥上进行的。

天桥在一片纵向生长的高楼的丛林中横向延展，像是遍布这个城市根系。它用一周五天的时间不疲倦地向CBD传递营养，终于在周末长出了喉咙。天桥这种潮汐变化，就好像有人把香港的底片规律性地浸入显影液之中：在市政功能性设施的纯粹外表之下，一次次将这个城市森林中那些长在地底的蘑菇翻掘出土。

啊！蘑菇们，趁着现在，好好倾吐和照拂彼此的菌丝吧！因为在重回那小小的隔间背后，你们仍然要听从森林的法则，仍然要收起彼此的孤独，仍然要为了效率选择沉默。
