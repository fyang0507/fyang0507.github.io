# Corporate Carrion Birds

When I started my first job at a private company, the business struck me as a finely tuned machine—running methodically and seemingly without any state. Within this rigorous system, everyone worked diligently, took pride in their profession, and never made mistakes.

A few months in, I began to glimpse the dynamics behind project progress. I saw how growth and development created mobility—shifts in positions and roles within the company (stateful). That was when I came to appreciate the fluidity of one's place in the organization, and I felt energized by the idea that through personal growth, one could shape both their own career and the company's products. As for those already established in their positions, I still assumed they were efficient and capable—how else could they have held onto their roles?

Later, I grew puzzled by the quality of deliverables from certain colleagues. At first, I simply thought they were slow or had the wrong approach. Eventually, though, I had to question their work ethic and competence. It turned out that completing a task in a substandard way was perfectly acceptable. Back then, I felt this was wrong—a betrayal of expectations all around.

Before long, I began to see my own manager's flaws. First, I discovered his staggering ignorance in matters of professional expertise. Then I watched him pilfer another team's achievements for his own reports, dressing it up with euphemisms like "leverage" and "influence"—the art of getting things done with limited resources. I realized that dedication bore no causal relationship to any favorable outcome.

As growth slowed, I saw it all: in divisional all-hands meetings, in company-wide assemblies convened by the CEO, in those grandiose announcements of strategies, plans, and reorganizations. I saw unworkable strategies, decisions made without any grassroots input, reform frameworks that skirted the real issues, the artful evasion of pressing questions, petty squabbling over trivial gains, and the unseemly triumphalism of political victors emerging from closed-door meetings. I glimpsed the deep-rooted maladies that plagued the entire system: inertia, compromise, selfishness, rent-seekers, bureaucratic culture, nepotism, factional infighting, and bloated organizations too entrenched to reform. I grew to loathe meetings convened solely to justify someone's existence, questions prepared only because answers already existed. Most of all, I despised those furtive whispers hidden in calendars as "private meetings"—the covert carving up of territories and the trading of favors.

As the saying goes: if you can't spot the sucker at the poker table, you're the sucker. By that point, I was certain I was no longer the sucker. I had finally been completely disenchanted with big corporations.

These companies exist only to perpetuate their own existence. Most of their internal organizations serve not their customers, but their own reproduction. They enshrine their founding purpose in a vision statement, then turn around and never look at it again.

The CEO of Surge AI, speaking of the ailments common to tech giants, once said: "90% of employees at these companies do utterly meaningless work." [1]

I don't know how radical that estimate is. Nor do I know to what extent AI will replace human labor. But I do know that growth can paper over all problems—and if it can't, just double the growth rate. For the curse that capitalism has left upon human society is this: in the absence of growth, people devolve into carrion birds. They don't mind eating rotten meat or the carcasses of their own kind, nor do they hesitate to brawl shamelessly over whatever fresh food remains.

A worker's attitude should follow the ancient wisdom: "When the state has the Way, be wise; when the state has lost the Way, play the fool." Of course, the option of not working for others exists as well. The question is: will AI usher in an era where such a choice is available to us? Or will it push that era even further out of reach?

[1] *20VC: Scaling to $1BN+ in Revenue with No Funding: Surge AI*, Apple Podcasts, July 2025.

职场食腐鸟

我在私企打第一份工那会儿，只觉得业务是一个精密的机器，它按部就班运转，似乎没有任何状态（stateless）。在这套严密的工序之中，每个人都勤奋做事，以自己的职业为傲，并且从不出差错。

几个月之后我开始看到关于项目进展的眉目，看到增长和发展如何在公司内带来职位和角色的流动（stateful），那时我开始认识到定位的模糊性，振奋于人可以通过自己的成长对自己的职业和公司的产品带来影响。但对于已经在既定位置上的人，我依然认为他们是高效、有能的，否则，很难解释他们怎样得以一直呆在那个位置。

再后来我逐渐诧异于某些同事项目交付的质量，我开头只是会觉得他们比较慢，或者方法不对；但后来我不得不怀疑起他们的工作态度和能力。——原来以一个不合格的方式完成任务也是可以接受的。那时我觉得这样不对，因为辜负了各种期待。

不久后我开始意识到自己老板的问题。我首先发现他在专业知识领域令人震惊的一无所知，然后又看到他如何窃取别组的成果写在自己的汇报里，并美其名曰：在有限资源下成事的”杠杆“和“影响力”。我发现尽心尽力不和任何好结局存在因果关系。

而随着增长放缓，我在大组的全员会上，在CEO召开的员工会议中，在那些冠冕堂皇的策略、计划、组织调整通告中，看到了明显行不通的战略、毫无基层反馈的决策、避重就轻的改革框架、堂皇的问题的回避、对蝇蝇小利的计较、以及闭门会议后的政治斗争胜者的弹冠相庆的丑态。我窥见整个系统的难以袚除的症结：惰性、妥协、自私、食利者、官场文化、裙带关系、派系斗争、尾大不掉的臃肿组织。我厌恶那些只为彰显存在感而被召开的讨论、因答案存在而准备的问题；我尤其厌恶那些藏在日程表里的被叫做“private meeting”的背人耳目的窃窃私语、版图瓜分和利益交换。

俗话说如果你在麻将桌上分不出谁是菜鸟，你就是那个菜鸟。那个时候我确信，我终于不是菜鸟，我终于对大企业彻底祛魅。
这些企业活着只是为了自己能够活下去。它里面的大部分组织不是服务于客户，而是服务于自我繁殖。他们把初心写进愿景，然后转过头再也不看它。

Surge AI的CEO谈到科技巨头的通病时曾说：“这些企业90%的员工做着毫无意义的工作。” [1]
这个估计有多激进我不知道，AI能代替人类工作到什么地步我也不知道，但我知道增长可以掩盖一切问题，如果不能，那就让增长率再涨一倍。因为资本主义留给人类社会的诅咒就是：没有增长的时候，人就会退化为食腐鸟。它们不介意吃烂掉的肉、同伴的尸体、或者在那些新鲜的食物前毫无尊严地群架。

打工人的态度应该“邦有道则知，邦无道则愚”，当然，不打工的选择也是有的。只是，AI会带给我们拥有这样选择的时代吗？或者我们离这样的时代反而更远？

[1] 20VC: Scaling to $1BN+ in Revenue with No Funding: Surge Al, Apple Podcast, 2025.07
