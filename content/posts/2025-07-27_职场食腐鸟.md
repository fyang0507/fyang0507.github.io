When I started my first job in corporate America, I imagined the business as a precision machine. It ran methodically, seemingly stateless. Within this rigorously ordered process, everyone worked hard, took pride in their profession, and never made mistakes.

A few months in, I began to get a clearer sense of how projects were progressing. I saw how growth and development set positions and roles in motion within the company, making it stateful. I began to understand how ambiguous a person's position could be, and I was thrilled by the thought that, through their own growth, someone could shape both their career and the company's product. But I still assumed that those already established in their positions had to be efficient and capable. Otherwise, it was hard to explain how they had managed to stay there.

Later still, I became increasingly puzzled by the quality of the work some colleagues delivered. At first, I simply thought they were slow or going about it the wrong way; eventually, I had to question their attitude and ability. Apparently, completing a task badly was acceptable too. I thought this was wrong because it betrayed all the expectations invested in the work.

Soon, I began to see the problems with my boss. First, I discovered his staggering ignorance of our professional field. Then I watched him steal another team's work for his own reports and dress it up as "leverage" and "impact"—getting things done with limited resources. I learned that doing your utmost bore no causal relationship to any good outcome.

As growth slowed, I saw it all in my broader organization's all-hands, in company meetings called by the CEO, and in every high-sounding announcement of a strategy, plan, or reorganization: strategies that plainly could not work; decisions made without any frontline feedback; reform frameworks designed to dodge the hard parts; grandiose evasions of serious problems; petty calculations over trifling gains; and the ugly preening of the faction that had won the political fight behind closed doors. I caught a glimpse of the pathology the entire system seemed incapable of exorcising: inertia, compromise, selfishness, rent-seekers, the culture of officialdom, nepotism, factional struggle, and a bloated organization too unwieldy to control. I hated discussions convened only to prove someone's relevance, and questions prepared because the answers already existed. Most of all, I hated the furtive whispers labeled "private meeting" on the calendar: the carving up of fiefdoms and the trading of favors, safely out of everyone else's hearing.

As the saying goes, if you can't tell who the rookie is at a mahjong table, you're the rookie. By then, I was certain: at last, I was no longer the rookie. At last, I was completely disenchanted with big corporations.
These companies live only to keep themselves alive. Most of the organizations within them exist not to serve customers but to reproduce themselves. They write their founding purpose into the vision statement, then turn around and never look at it again.

When discussing this common pathology of tech giants, the CEO of Surge AI once estimated that 90 percent of the employees at such companies were doing meaningless work. [1]
I don't know how radical that estimate is. Nor do I know how much human labor AI can replace. But I do know that growth can hide every problem. If it can't, just double the growth rate again. This is the curse capitalism has left human society: without growth, people degenerate into vultures. They do not mind feeding on rotting flesh or their companions' corpses, or brawling among themselves without a shred of dignity even in front of fresh food.

A wage worker should live by this: "When the state has the Way, be wise; when it lacks the Way, play the fool." Of course, opting out of wage labor is also a choice. But will AI bring us to an age when that choice is truly ours? Or are we moving farther away from such an age?

我在私企打第一份工那会儿，只觉得业务是一个精密的机器，它按部就班运转，似乎没有任何状态（stateless）。在这套严密的工序之中，每个人都勤奋做事，以自己的职业为傲，并且从不出差错。

几个月之后我开始看到关于项目进展的眉目，看到增长和发展如何在公司内带来职位和角色的流动（stateful），那时我开始认识到定位的模糊性，振奋于人可以通过自己的成长对自己的职业和公司的产品带来影响。但对于已经在既定位置上的人，我依然认为他们是高效、有能的，否则，很难解释他们怎样得以一直呆在那个位置。

再后来我逐渐诧异于某些同事项目交付的质量，我开头只是会觉得他们比较慢，或者方法不对；但后来我不得不怀疑起他们的工作态度和能力。——原来以一个不合格的方式完成任务也是可以接受的。那时我觉得这样不对，因为辜负了各种期待。

不久后我开始意识到自己老板的问题。我首先发现他在专业知识领域令人震惊的一无所知，然后又看到他如何窃取别组的成果写在自己的汇报里，并美其名曰：在有限资源下成事的”杠杆“和“影响力”。我发现尽心尽力不和任何好结局存在因果关系。

而随着增长放缓，我在大组的全员会上，在CEO召开的员工会议中，在那些冠冕堂皇的策略、计划、组织调整通告中，看到了明显行不通的战略、毫无基层反馈的决策、避重就轻的改革框架、堂皇的问题的回避、对蝇蝇小利的计较、以及闭门会议后的政治斗争胜者的弹冠相庆的丑态。我窥见整个系统的难以袚除的症结：惰性、妥协、自私、食利者、官场文化、裙带关系、派系斗争、尾大不掉的臃肿组织。我厌恶那些只为彰显存在感而被召开的讨论、因答案存在而准备的问题；我尤其厌恶那些藏在日程表里的被叫做“private meeting”的背人耳目的窃窃私语、版图瓜分和利益交换。

俗话说如果你在麻将桌上分不出谁是菜鸟，你就是那个菜鸟。那个时候我确信，我终于不是菜鸟，我终于对大企业彻底祛魅。
这些企业活着只是为了自己能够活下去。它里面的大部分组织不是服务于客户，而是服务于自我繁殖。他们把初心写进愿景，然后转过头再也不看它。

Surge AI的CEO谈到科技巨头的通病时曾说：“这些企业90%的员工做着毫无意义的工作。” [1]
这个估计有多激进我不知道，AI能代替人类工作到什么地步我也不知道，但我知道增长可以掩盖一切问题，如果不能，那就让增长率再涨一倍。因为资本主义留给人类社会的诅咒就是：没有增长的时候，人就会退化为食腐鸟。它们不介意吃烂掉的肉、同伴的尸体、或者在那些新鲜的食物前毫无尊严地群架。

打工人的态度应该“邦有道则知，邦无道则愚”，当然，不打工的选择也是有的。只是，AI会带给我们拥有这样选择的时代吗？或者我们离这样的时代反而更远？
