## Prologue

If the 2020s began with people being jolted awake by a fault line, then spending the next few years trying to adjust to this new normal, this year should count as the beginning of the era when nobody even bothers pretending anymore. The greatest change has been psychological. A few years ago, we thought the world was about to fall apart. We worried about the future, so we were always trying to stop something and, while we were at it, looking wistfully back at the old days. We were powerless as events unfolded, but we still imagined we could wait them out.

Perhaps everyone had finally had enough of sleeping with their heads buried under the covers. We all lost patience with that half-dead doze. Some grew restless to do something; others simply rolled up their sleeves and began pushing the boulder. Yet the days that had seemed to hang by mere threads now felt impossibly heavy. Was the knife too blunt, or the net too tight? Everyone believed they were the one being ignored, restrained, insulted, or harmed. Faced with the shackles clamped around us, we all craned our necks toward the shell that seemed ready to break open.

What comes after the shell breaks? Who the fuck cares.


## I

I have a theory: the artichoke became food only because the people trimming it and the people eating it were two different groups.

Before you reach the tender heart, you have to strip away the tough outer leaves, the thorny tips and pointed crown, the fuzzy choke, the fibrous skin of the stem, and the hard base. By the end, an entire green lotus bud has been whittled down to one tiny lotus seat.

When Elon Musk assembled the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), the federal government must have looked much the same to him: waiting obediently to die, with very little of it actually useful.

Musk's romantic fantasy is understandable. Once you treat government as nothing more than an inorganic, functional machine—and what a bloated machine it is—every process inside it can be abstracted into a shortest-path problem and radically optimized for efficiency. Within eighty hours of its creation, DOGE had efficiently canceled forty million dollars in contracts; within a little over half a year, it had cut roughly two hundred thousand federal jobs.[1, 2]

The people Musk put to work at DOGE were not seasoned insiders but brilliant young engineers with almost no experience.[3] They knew systems and quantification, and they believed that in any complex, high-dimensional, abstract problem, gradient descent could solve everything.

The method rests on an approximation: a person is a resource; a person is a component; a person is a geometric symbol in an input-output ratio.

But people are still people.

And where there are people, politics follows.

Musk has believed in and exploited first-principles thinking his entire life, to astonishing commercial success. But this may have been the first time he himself became the first principle. Someone had figured him out: put Musk on a pedestal beneath a grand title that looked vaguely like “minister,” and he would dance with excitement, hack through the mess, and offend crowds of people he never cared about anyway.

It may be conspiratorial to say that Musk was deliberately used as a weapon. A more realistic possibility is that certain “enablers” wanted his radical methods to tear open a breach at lightning speed, then planned to let events run their course if all went well. That did not happen. Once public fury began to boil, the enablers took the referee's chair with an air of “let me be fair here,” diverted attention, and shifted the cost-cutting plan into a less visible mode.

But hey, you and I still thought it was all the fault of that goddamn Musk!

In *Life Is Elsewhere*, Milan Kundera wrote: “Youth, poetry, and revolution are one and the same.” All three arise from idealistic passion; all three distort the substance of life through lyricism. Many revolutions failed not because of enemies, blockades, or repression, but because they were too romantic and too pure—because they took ideas too seriously and people not seriously enough.

So youth, poetry, and the Department of Government Efficiency are also one and the same. Exactly the same.


## II

OpenAI's most powerful man, Sam Altman, once said he liked to “build in public.”[4] He dismissed old-fashioned annual launches and the practice of saving up for one giant announcement, so OpenAI created a new corporate culture: ship without warning and ruin a rival's launch event.[5]

But those tricks belonged to a year ago, when OpenAI was lonely at the top and everyone else combined still could not beat it. It had the leisure to invent new ways of provoking people. Altman solemnly said, “We named it ChatGPT and not a person's name very intentionally”—which, in his telling, was meant to keep people from getting addicted to this phenomenal product with a cold codename.[6]

OpenAI is no longer so relaxed. More precisely, it is panicking its ass off. Not long ago, with growth slowing, ChatGPT loosened its restrictions on adult content. Altman's response was that the company was “not the elected moral police of the world.”[7] Then Google released Gemini 3, and the whole company sounded a code red over its survival.[8] At the product level, ChatGPT now has to defend growth and retention—which is to say it would love nothing more than for you to spend every day getting horny over a cold codename.

I have run through all this news to make one point: Altman really is one hell of a flexible loudmouth. But think one step further and you realize that all three men leading America's big three in AI—Sam, Dario, and Musk—are eager to put themselves forward, strut around, and say something shocking.

Sometimes the coincidence makes me laugh out loud. At this turning point in human history, the figures taking the stage exposed their cores so early, and those cores conform so perfectly to the **stereotypes** of similar characters in fictional worlds.

(Let me repeat something I wrote in 2022: “To anthropomorphize with the greatest possible charity: America is like Gendo Ikari, with a righteous plan known to all and a private scheme behind it; China is like Asuka, who believes it is the elite's duty to save the ignorant masses. Neither is easy to deal with.”)

Sam and Dario are the Gendo Ikari and Asuka of AI.

### 1.0

Sam's hypocrisy is obvious to everyone. Just as conspicuous are his political cunning and personal charm. The famous venture capitalist Paul Graham long ago described Altman's magic: “You could parachute him into an island full of cannibals and come back in 5 years and he'd be the king.”[9] Indeed, during OpenAI's internal purge in 2023, a man publicly denounced by the board as dishonest somehow returned to the center of power as though nothing had happened. Anyone who can manipulate politics with such ease obviously has the skill to match his ambition. But what is Altman's ambition?

In the middle of the year, Sam published “The Gentle Singularity” on his personal blog.[10] “We are past the event horizon; the takeoff has started,” he claimed. Given our benign trajectory, humanity could move safely and smoothly into the future through good governance and a consensus on values. It makes perfect sense, and not one word of it should be believed. Gendo Ikari's story tells us that although you can never quite tell what this man is thinking, one thing is certain: what he wants is absolutely not what he says. I do not believe in the gentle singularity. More than that: in any future where he remains center stage, I should not permit myself even that hope.

### 2.0

To describe Dario's personality more precisely, we need to bring in a second fictional universe. The more accurate formulation is this: Dario is Asuka after a stint at **Red Coast Base**.

Dario himself says almost nothing about his time at Baidu Research. But there is no way private hostility—or at least private emotion—played no part when, at a moment that demanded no such public alignment, he announced that Chinese companies and their overseas subsidiaries would be barred from using Claude,[11] or when he solemnly proclaimed that Anthropic had foiled a Chinese government plot to conduct cyberattacks with AI.[12]

It is hard to associate Dario himself with political maneuvering. He was not even invited to Trump's White House dinner on AI and technological sovereignty.[13] Meanwhile, he blurted out in an interview that one-fifth of entry-level white-collar jobs would disappear within one to five years.[14] (The wily old Altman was inside the White House flattering Trump, having also written the blog full of lies mentioned above.) Dario plainly has little experience in commercial warfare, and I would bet he is proud of that inexperience.

That is Dario's arrogance: almost everything he says, he says sincerely. He sincerely believes that no AI but his is safe enough. He sincerely believes the dull-witted public needs to be awakened by a thunderclap. He believes the evolution of humanity is his personal responsibility. Until he learns to be smooth, you can believe anything he says. But you should also know that he is the kind of person who would pull the lever in the trolley problem without hesitation and never suffer a pang of conscience. He would believe that the extraordinary people chosen to grow the new world naturally deserve rights beyond the norms of existing society.[15]

(As for what happened at Red Coast Base? Before the Advent, he may keep it all to himself.)

### 3.0

Once we have Red Coast and Ye Wenjie, how could we leave out the triumphalist Zhang Beihai? Electric cars, interplanetary rockets, xAI built to fight OpenAI, lavish funding for Trump's campaign: every one of Musk's actions is for the future, every stroke of madness and genius born from despair at humanity.

### 4.0

Let us vote democratically! Between hypocrisy, arrogance, and despair, choose the habitat best suited to humankind.

Or would you rather turn your gaze eastward?


## III

From DeepSeek's thunderclap at the beginning of the year, through Alibaba's Qwen models dominating the rankings, to Kimi K2 Thinking's long-horizon reasoning and tool use, it is now an industry consensus that China has some of the world's best open-source large language models. These results were achieved under severe constraints on compute. I find that encouraging, but not surprising: when it comes to tightening our belts and fighting our way back, we do possess unrivaled historical experience.

I know another way of living with a tightened belt. Not long ago, I watched quite a few guides to low-cost urban survival, the practice known as “wall-hanging”: living on odd jobs and day wages, holding consumption to the absolute minimum, and spending your entire life in power-saving mode. Among young people in somewhat better circumstances, another trend has emerged: “free ingredient mystery boxes,” or collecting vegetables that fall from wholesale trucks or look too ugly to sell as market stalls open or close.[16] Videos of both can now be found online, which means their existence is an officially permitted expression within the language of “consumption downgrading.”

There are more explicit ways to talk about tightening belts, such as: “Manual laborers are sexually repressed Android users.” Every noun in that sentence opens onto its own black-comic theoretical framework, so nearly every hot social issue in China this year falls within its range.

There are many ways to refuse to sink on a tightened belt. They may take the form of conversations, follow-along interviews, first-person records, or clipped livestreams. Whether they lean toward self-narration, experience, or entertainment, at least someone is speaking—even if that speech is deconstructive or mocking. And whether they set out to laugh at themselves, ask a question, or expose something, the fact that these videos become popular tells us where attention is gathering.

I cannot say more about this. I only hope that some of our economic and spiritual deprivation can be eliminated, and that these antagonisms can be reconciled with greater tenderness.


## IV

I do not know a single one of the young people who film themselves scavenging vegetables at markets. That makes what I just said sound phony, and I entirely accept the criticism: everything I described above came to me secondhand. What follows, however, is based on fieldwork. If you want to become an adult, do not go to Silicon Valley.

I first visited California in 2018. I was a naive kid fresh from reading *The Enigma of Silicon Valley* and *The Crest of the Wave*,[17] and my fellow graduate students and I all dreamed of going to Silicon Valley. The trip left me disillusioned, because I decided that the technology center of the world and I were fundamentally incompatible. I wrote at the time: “These tech giants carve the territory into concession-like enclaves, each with an air of autonomy. The indifference to their surroundings born of this sheltered isolation once again displays the refined elitism I despise most.”

This year, Silicon Valley exported a concept called “taste” to the world. The idea is that, in the age of AI, how one uses tools will matter less and less—AI can stitch anything together for you—while the decisive difference between things will be human taste. Here are a few moments of exquisite Silicon Valley taste: taking home enough food from the free company lunch to feed yourself and your entire extended family at dinner; bringing company plates and bowls home so you never have to buy tableware; buying a Tesla but charging it exclusively at work; maintaining a detailed taxonomy of points and cash-back benefits across more than a dozen credit cards, and taking pride in it.

For elite employees paid far more than the general public, these habits seem rather petty. I am not the only person who finds them ridiculous. But most critiques then follow the path of “vision” and “aesthetics,” until everything is blamed on “small-town test-taking strivers with no humanities education.” I want to offer another view.

Once mature products and advertising systems have passed their high-growth phase, Silicon Valley's data-driven decision framework begins to “overoptimize.” To meet a metric, engineering teams rack their brains to push click-through rate, time spent, daily active users, and so on toward a limit visible only in the statistics. Apple's products are “no longer cool.”[18] Nobody would dream of using something as antiquated as Facebook. Yet quarter after quarter, these companies' core products still contribute steady, marginal growth to earnings. Silicon Valley engineers have brought this system into their daily lives: benefits, cash back, subsidies, and taxes are optimized to the limit, until life becomes one enormous exercise in reward hacking.

Quantifiability sits at the core of the Silicon Valley elite mind, and it also shapes the Valley's appetite for comparison. Just as you look like a hick walking down a Shanghai street without a bottle of pour-over coffee, in Silicon Valley your identity comes under suspicion if your T-shirt and backpack bear no company logo. You probably do not work at a big tech company. If you do, how could you resist displaying your badge at all times? After one quick glance, the stranger passing by can be modeled immediately as a function of salary, title, and address. Anything beyond that is irrelevant, and truly cannot be seen. Sometimes it is seen, but there is no reason to admit it.

Silicon Valley loves persuading people outside software to “learn to code,” which tells you that software engineering occupies the gleaming top rung of its ladder. Engineers from Chinese backgrounds, however, complain of a career ceiling: they can perfect their technical ability but never break through some invisible promotion line. At the same time, they watch more and more people of Indian background rise along the senior management track. To be fair, this is not about so-called technical competence. It really is about politics and manipulation—flattery, nepotism, games of talk. But these things do not take place in public. They do not appear among the official evaluation criteria of the performance system, so they are treated as dirty.

Silicon Valley has a simple moral code. Its most naked power games unfold there, yet rank-and-file engineers are well protected—or, you could say, well deceived. They cannot see power circulating, so they believe in a “clean” meritocracy: only achievements captured by rankings and scoring systems count as legitimate excellence.

This is not entirely their fault. Silicon Valley's “elite” branding and its student mentality are two sides of the same coin. In values and lived experience, the Valley is terrifyingly homogeneous.

I have heard that, even after the pandemic, many Chinese universities continue to impose a closed-management system resembling high school: cards at the gates, approval required to leave, students' daily lives strictly confined within the campus walls, with little chance for friction with society as it actually exists. Silicon Valley offices resemble campuses; three free meals a day resemble the cafeteria; periodic reviews resemble semester report cards. Big-tech employees live inside a preset schedule: commuter shuttle, buffet lunch, gym after work, then the Bay Area's three clichés on weekends.[19] You can earn far more than your peers in such a system while rarely having to face a genuinely public situation.

Historically, wealth and power generally match. The more money you have, the louder your voice. As people move upward, they naturally assume more social roles and acquire greater influence and authority. The strangeness of Silicon Valley is that a substantial number of people possess immense economic power while apparently not existing in society at all. A poverty of diversity has turned the place into an echo chamber inside an ivory tower. Silicon Valley looks like a continuing-education institute for practitioners of polished self-interest.

When Chinese internet giants lay people off, they say the employees have “graduated.” It is cruel, but also really fucking precise.


## V

Some people are no longer students but refuse to graduate; another group of students is desperate to drop out. Venture capital firm a16z says AI has created a structural opportunity, making 2025 the best opportunity for young founders,[20] while the share of current college students or recent graduates in Y Combinator has shot from 10 percent to 30 percent.[21]

They have brought some genuine student behavior to Silicon Valley, such as not drinking at parties—some founders are under the legal drinking age. But more than that, they have brought the fearlessness and appetite for a gamble peculiar to the young.

You might not expect that, alongside open-source large language models, Chinese technology companies also exported the 996 work culture this year: 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week.[22] Silicon Valley's AI startups have abandoned big tech's concern for work-life balance and benefits. Nearly everyone is in the office at least five days a week. Founders tell employees plainly: if your passion cannot sustain an eighty-hour workweek, you should not join us.[23]

There is, of course, a logic to this madness. AI products have not yet built their moats. The land grab is raging, and the giants are circling. Everyone in the game believes the winner will take all, so beneath the accelerationism lies a genuine fear for survival: if you are not greedy enough, you become food.

In China, manual laborers who go all in seem to feel the whole social and cultural climate pushing from behind. Behind these young founders lies an equally cold reality: AI replacing white-collar work; STEM unemployment in the United States exceeding humanities unemployment for the first time; large companies reducing hiring across the board; entry-level jobs disappearing. Yet the forces creating this climate are by no means limited to anxiety.

Even among founders, Cluely's CEO stands out. He first became known for recording himself using AI software he had written to “cheat” his way through an Amazon interview. Columbia University expelled him, and he rode the attention into founding Cluely. From the beginning, the company has prized internet instinct: one hiring requirement was at least one hundred thousand social-media followers. It bought a giant billboard in Times Square, but filled it with just three lines of black lowercase text on white:

> hi i'm roy im 21
> this was very expensive
> pls buy my thing

This year, I listened to a podcast featuring Roy.[24] On his blog, he says his genius lies in understanding the mechanics of internet virality, while “those middle-aged geezers only post dry shit on social media.” As he said it, he had the look of a scammer watching older people fumble with their feature phones:

These people are rich and know nothing. Why not fleece them?

The young blood and audacity of Silicon Valley founders deserve praise. Or put it this way: if you have barely entered college when rich people tell you that degrees are useless,[25] then watch an endless wave of layoffs and graduates tumbling straight into unemployment, how are you supposed to save yourself? If you still possess confidence, you will probably embrace a form of agency built around “refusing mediocrity,” one in which your identity has to be confirmed by severing yourself from the mediocre masses.

I have read in more than one place that many Silicon Valley founders are fans of Ayn Rand.[26] I read *The Fountainhead* when I was young, but my own ambition was never large enough, so I was moved only by its solitude, independence, and resolve. When I returned to my notes this year, I realized that Rand reduces the “ideal man” to an embodiment of willpower and originality. Evil, by contrast, is given a concrete subject: you can point to a person, a group, or a system and say that it obstructs humanity's march toward greatness. In the introduction to *The Fountainhead*, she wrote: “In every generation, only a few men continue to understand and fully to achieve the proper stature of man—and the rest betray it.” This framework, with its crisp division between good and evil, is perfectly suited to a Silicon Valley with a special fetish for disruption.

Here lies the problem. In the stories these AI founders tell, there is the close-up of “My heart is in the work” and the long shot of “AI will reshape everything.” There is the justice of devoting oneself completely to technological progress, and the evil of trying to obstruct the advance of AI applications. Most of the gray territory in the middle may as well not exist. High-profile startup Mercor proclaims a mission of “unlocking human potential,”[27] then turns around and cuts the pay of its data annotators,[28] as though their potential had become a little too great.

We could summarize the whole thing as one more episode of “capitalist hypocrisy and cruelty.” But I see it more as Kua Fu chasing the sun—the giant who pursued it until he died of thirst. These young people genuinely believe in the distant vision of AI radically improving humanity's fate. But the vision has no connection to society as it exists; more than that, everything now happening in society seems to threaten their utopia.

This has happened more than once before: we will benefit humanity, but first we have to destroy you.


## VI

The lack of connection is at the root of everything.

Silicon Valley's elite move directly from school into big tech, or directly into being a founder. They see people as abstractions: labor costs, head count, membership conversion, customer acquisition. But in their own lives, they rarely have to face more flesh-and-blood people. They fundamentally lack the sensation of feet planted on earth—what one might call “grassroots experience.” And this is absolutely not just a problem for Silicon Valley or the Department of Government Efficiency.

The individualization brought by modernity, combined with social media's projection of distant vistas straight onto the individual, has produced a kind of weightlessness: a rupture in the sense of control and a confusion of identity. Where is the boundary between person and society? What lies between a person and the world? Some describe this condition as “the missing middle distance.”

I gave this year's retrospective the subtitle “Those Who Refuse to Sink.” When I say refusing to sink, what I really mean is rebuilding the middle distance: beyond grand narratives and personal experience, seeing once again the concrete faces of other people.

But I am not nostalgic. I know the old middle distance was no good either.

This year, I read several sociological books about rural China. That middle distance was deeply stifling, like an airtight wall forcing “people to direct all their attention toward the village right in front of them... The meaning of life becomes winning the envy and applause of the village, with little motivation to improve one's life. In this culture, interpersonal relations weigh too heavily; they squeeze the individual out.”[29] That middle distance was pathological. As Chinese urbanization broke apart traditional relations, it quickly swept them into history's dustbin—though not quite cleanly.

In the middle distance, the previous generation's problems concerned conscience and courage: do you dare to leave, and once you have, can you hold yourself back? Ours concern hope and honesty. I think we are increasingly able to tolerate ourselves and increasingly unable to tolerate other people. We are too good at reducing everything to “none of my fucking business” and “none of your fucking business.” At the slightest friction in values, we turn away from everything we share. We accept loneliness because we assume no form of common life is worth the time it takes to cultivate and repair.

Technology has given us the freedom to block, the freedom to leave the group, the freedom to escape. Rebuilding demands more patience. I do not want to pretend things have nothing to do with me. I do not want to pass off detachment as elegance. I do not want to say the first to care is the one who loses. My thought is very simple: I need other people, and I need other people to need me.


## VII

No one can deny that we are living through a historical cycle of profound change. The unprecedented events alone are almost too many to follow. Yet it is just as easy to imagine that, in *A Short History of XX* two hundred years from now, every life lived by our generation will be compressed into a handful of sentences.

Historical writing requires a distilled thesis and a commanding view. Everyone understands that dimensionality reduction always brings information loss. I feel nothing about that in the abstract. But when I imagine facing such compression myself, I find it hard to breathe. Statistics is uncanny. A common denominator is really nothing at all. The parts averaged away, the parts represented out of existence, are the true substance of life. Only the flattened fluctuations of the everyday deserve to be called life itself.

No one can deny that history's moments of “phase transition” always arrive at points of structural imbalance, and circumstance plays a substantial role. California was the first state in American history to grant women property rights. It was not because the legislators had suffered an attack of conscience, but because without such rights, the migrant men arriving en masse would have become an increasingly destabilizing force, and an already extreme gender imbalance would have worsened.[30] The Emancipation Proclamation came from Lincoln's moral convictions, but even more from his wartime strategy—the emancipation policy was a means of winning the war. As he said: “My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery.”

But if we stop asking questions there—if we decide that the wheel of history rolls forward on its own, casually correcting a few injustices along the way and randomly selecting a few lucky spectators to hit the jackpot and become heroes—then we fall into the aerial-view trap again. We reduce everything to mechanical structures, as though the course of history bore no relation to human agency.

For a long stretch last year, I was terrified. I thought: how miserable, how deflating, to live through history's garbage time, spending one's days inside a span destined to be forgotten. Now I understand. There is too much history books do not bother to mention, so most of history is garbage time to begin with. But that has a positive meaning.

These unremembered stretches carry uncertain trends. They hold contradictory forms of new life sprouting through the everyday, multiple forces already in motion. What they represent is not a fated ending but formation itself.

This year, I visited three new places: Hawaii, Sacramento, and New Orleans. On the surface, they are merely scattered American place names. In some *Short History of the United States*, they might be condensed into three unrelated phrases: vacation destination, birthplace of the Gold Rush, home of jazz.

But I found folds hidden inside all three names, folds that could be unfolded in a polynomial expansion. Hawaii was the epicenter of the attack on Pearl Harbor, but it also stages the most authentically American fireworks display. Sacramento uses a freeway to sever the city from its riverbank, as though afraid to admit that it once lived by water. The lower Mississippi resembles a fish skeleton: seven hundred rib-like roads branching from its spine still lead toward the seven hundred plantations that multiplied here two centuries ago. After these wounds receded, the fertile ground continued to struggle. It remains one of America's poorest and most polluted regions.

Across these journeys, I strung together an understanding of many things: how national memory is distorted and consumed; how capital and development plans break or conceal the historical grain of cities; how thunderous proclamations of progress are systematically obstructed; how economics, class, and culture continually become sources of dependence or shackles across different times and places.

Yes, from three thousand feet above the ground, we can make any number of deterministic claims—for example, that the Americas were colonized because their continental axis ran north to south.[31] But only by entering the streets, seeing walls peel, smelling the air found nowhere else, and hearing the meaningless static between monument and monument can we be moved, and can we understand the true scale of these folds.

History is gradual by nature. Iron the folds flat and they open into a long slope with no end in sight, not a step waiting to trip us—and at every present moment, we do indeed stand at the beginning of that slope.


## VIII

To understand history, I think, is mainly to understand its inertia. Epochal events do not appear from nowhere, and they do not transform everyone's life overnight. They are more like buoys in a harbor: sailors use them to find their position, but the currents beneath the surface never stop moving.

In 1928, the Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act passed, and British women gained the vote on equal terms. But for many days before that, amid advancing industrialization and urbanization, women had already been working in factories, hospitals, and offices. Their growing participation in the relations of social production gradually widened the scope in which rights had to apply. The Act merely gave this current its belated name.

But a promise on paper does not mean a happy ending. Women's rights did not fully improve with the vote. Unequal pay for equal work, invisible career ceilings, stereotypes of the “strong woman” and the “good woman”: these problems remain unresolved even today. The definition of “feminism” itself has been diminished in public discourse, from a vanguard identity into a story of conflict between the sexes. The year 1928 is a vivid marker in the movement for women's rights. A long uphill road led to it, and an equally long tail trails behind.

In 1965, civil-rights legislation dismantled legal segregation. Many of the Martin Luther Kings who began boycotts and sit-ins, then were beaten, arrested, and executed, did not live to see that day. But without decades of people throwing themselves into organizing and mobilization, race would never have reached the public agenda in the first place.

Afterward, overt discrimination was no longer accepted by the mainstream, but suspicious neighborhood boundaries, biased lending policies, and disparities in sentencing persisted. These aftereffects would require more Martins, and those Martins might no longer have names of their own.

So when I say “garbage time has a positive meaning,” I do not mean that I am waiting for some “formation” and hoping it suits my tastes. I hope to adopt an orientation toward action. I want to write the stories of years that have not yet settled into history, because what people actually do determines which way the moment of “phase transition” will finally tip.

(I suspect Foucault wanted a productive account of power, rather than a purely negative and repressive one, from a similar concern with agency.)

I keep writing in order to make quick sketches of the details against a purely aerial narrative. To act only as a recorder carries its own passivity—as though I could do nothing but lie on the riverbed, watching the glimmering light drift across my body.[32] In the end, because I live inside history, I cannot become detached and panoramic.

I want always to write from the perspective of an ordinary humanist. Inevitably, among the things I witness or hear, some deserve sympathy, praise, grief, or contempt, and I will take a clear position toward them. But I do not want to confine myself to the framework of victimhood. I do not want clean binary oppositions. I do not want to preach heroism or construct figures resembling gods and demons. At the end of the story, I do not want to seal everything inside retribution, the reversal of extremes, the return of fortune after adversity, or the justice of heaven.

History always remembers direction and structure, I think. But people live on the ground, not along the curvature of the sky. From the ground, what we see is unstable and unresolved: an everyday life in which good and evil are mixed. At this scale, an orientation toward action asks us to remain sensitive, to welcome being moved, and to refuse to grow accustomed.

I would like to say I write in order to refuse forgetting, but that too is a heroic formulation. So all I can say is: I think, therefore I am—except that by “am,” I mean not that I exist, but where I ought to stand.

在成熟产品和广告系统上，硅谷数据驱动的决策框架在度过高增长期后就会开始“过度优化”：为了完成一个指标，工程团队要费尽心思把点击率、停留时长、日活等等，推到一个仅有统计意义的极限。苹果的产品已经”不酷了” [18]，没有人会再使用Facebook这种老掉牙的玩意儿，但几乎每个季度，这些公司的财报里的核心产品仍在贡献稳定的小幅增长。硅谷工程师把这套体系带到了自己的起居里：把福利、返现、补贴、缴税都优化到极致，然后生活就成了一场大型reward hacking。

可量化性是硅谷精英思维的核心，这也体现在硅谷热爱攀比的氛围里。就像你走在上海的街上没有拿一瓶手冲咖啡就显得像个乡毋宁，在硅谷如果你的T恤和背包如果没有公司的Logo也被人质疑身份：你大概不是大厂的吧。如果是大厂的，你怎么能忍住不把工牌随时亮出来呢？在这样迅速的一瞥之后，这个擦肩而过的人可以被立刻建模：一个关于薪资、职称、住址的函数。除此以外的东西不重要，也确实看不到。有时是看到了，但又没理由承认。

硅谷热衷于说服非软件类工作者“转码”，这说明软件工程师在硅谷是一个台阶鲜亮的顶端。但中国背景的工程师，又会抱怨自己遇到职业天花板：技术做到极致，却始终无法突破某条晋升线。与此同时，他们又亲眼看到，高层管理线上，印度裔背景的人愈发地多起来。公正地讲，这不是所谓技术能力，而确实有关政治和操弄，是马屁，是裙带关系、是嘴皮子游戏——但这些东西并不出现在公开场合，不是明面上的绩效系统里的能力评审标准，所以它们被认为是些脏东西。

硅谷有一套朴素的道德标准。尽管这里上演着最赤裸的权力游戏，但基层的工程师被保护得很好（也可以说是被欺骗得很好），他们看不见权力的流通，所以他们信奉着一种“干净”的优绩主义：能够被排行榜和评分系统捕捉到的成就，才被视为正当的优秀。

这不能完全怪他们，因为硅谷的“精英”招牌和它的“学生气”本就是硬币的两面，因为在价值观和生活经验层面，硅谷同质化得可怕。

我听说，疫情之后，中国不少大学至今仍维持着类似高中的封闭管理：校门刷卡，外出审批，学生的日常生活被严格控制在院墙之内，很难与真正地社会发生摩擦。而硅谷的园区像校园，免费三餐像食堂，周期评审像学期成绩单；大厂员工在一套预设好的日程里生活：通勤班车、自助午餐、下班健身房、周末湾区三俗 [19]。你可以在这样的体系里拿到远超同龄人的收入，却很少需要面对真正的公共场景。

历史上看，一般而言，财富和权力是相匹配的。你有多少钱，你就能说多大声的话。一个人在向上流动的过程中，自然会承担更多的社会角色，获得更大的影响力和话语权。但硅谷的奇特就在于，这里有相当部分的人有着极其可观的经济力量，但在社会上这群人好像完全不存在。多样性的匮乏，让这里成为一个象牙塔里的回音壁，所以硅谷看上去像是为精致利己主义者开办的继续教育机构。

中国互联网大厂裁员的时候说“毕业了”，这很残忍，但也很他妈的精确。


（五）

有人不是学生了却老不毕业，但另一群学生却着急着辍学。风投机构a16z称AI带来结构性的机遇，所以2025年是年轻人最好的创业机会 [20]，而YC孵化器中在校大学生或应届毕业生的占比从10%骤增至30% [21]。

这群人给硅谷带来了些许正儿八经的学生气，比如聚会不饮酒（有的创业者不满法定年龄）；但更多的是一种独属年轻人的勇猛和赌性。

你也许想不到，中国的科技公司今年除了出口开源大模型以外，也出口了996的工作文化 [22]。硅谷的AI创业公司一改大厂对工作生活平衡及福利制度的重视，几乎是全员至少五天坐办公室；创始人会明确告诉员工：如果你的激情不能支持你一周工作80小时的话，你最好还是不要加入我们 [23]。

这种疯狂当然有它的道理。AI产品的护城河还没有建立起来，圈地运动如火如荼，各大巨头虎视眈眈；参与这场游戏的所有人都笃信赢家通吃，所以这种加速主义背后也确实是有危急存亡的担忧：不足够贪婪，就会成为食物。

在中国，“力工梭哈”背后似有一种社会文化的整体环境在隐隐推动；而这群年轻的创业者背后，也有着冰冷的现实：人工智能对白领工作的取代，美国理科失业率首次高于文科失业率，大型企业整体缩招，初级岗位的消失……但形成这种气候的原因绝不仅仅来自忧虞。

在众多创业者中，Cluely的CEO也算得上个性鲜明的一个。他最早为人所知是录播用自己编写的AI软件“作弊”通过亚马逊的面试，最后被哥伦比亚大学开除，然后他就借势创立了Cluely。从成立初始，这个公司就一直重视网感，比如说它的招聘条件里要求申请人社交媒体的粉丝数不少于10万个。它在寸土寸金的时代广场曾买下巨幅广告位，但只用白底黑字的纯小写写了三行字：“你好我是罗伊我今年21了/这玩意儿死贵/求你们来买我的东西吧。”

我今年听了罗伊作嘉宾的一场播客 [24]。他在博客里说自己的天才就在于深谙互联网的病毒传播之道，而“那些中登只会在社交媒体上发些干巴巴的东西”。他说这话的时候那个眼神就像诈骗分子看长辈们用老年机一样：

这群人又有钱又什么都不懂，为什么不宰他们一刀呢？

硅谷的创业者们年轻气盛，这是值得嘉许的事情。或者说，如果你刚考入大学就听到有钱人说“学历无用” [25]，接着看到层出不穷的裁员和毕业即失业的窘境，你又该如何自救？如果你对自己还保有信心的话，大概率就会进入一种“拒绝平庸”的能动性里，而这个状态下你需要通过与庸众割席来获得身份确认。

我在不只一个地方看到，硅谷的创业者中不少是安·兰德的书迷 [26]。我年轻时看过《源泉》，但因我个人的野心不够大，所以仅仅让触动停留在了对孤独、遗世独立和决心的欣赏上。今年我再次翻看摘抄，我才发现安·兰德会把所谓“完人”简化成精神力、独创性的代表；而与之相对的邪恶则有某种主体性：就是说你可以指向一个人、一个团体、或一个系统，然后说，它在人类的伟大进程里扮演了绊脚石的作用。《源泉》的序言中她写道：“每一代人中，只有少数人能够完全理解和完全实现人类的才能，而其余的人都背叛了它。”这种可以明快划分正邪的框架，正适用于对颠覆有着特殊迷恋的硅谷。

问题就在这里。在这些AI创业者的叙事里，有“My heart is in the work”的近景，也有“AI重塑一切”的远景；有把个人全身心献给科技进步的正义，也有妄图阻碍智能应用发展的邪恶。至于这中间的大部分灰色地带，就跟完全不存在似的。明星创业公司Mercor一边说自己的使命是“解放人类的潜力” [27]，一边转头就给数据标注员降薪 [28]，就像是嫌他们潜力太大似的。

我们尽可以把整件事概括为又一个“资本家的伪善和无情”，但我更觉得这是个夸父逐日的例子：这些年轻人是真的相信AI能大幅改善人类命运的远景，但这个愿景与现实社会没有联结，更一进步说，这个社会目前发生的一切好像都在对那种乌托邦造成威胁。

这种事之前也发生过几次：我们造福人类，但是要消灭你们。


（六）

缺乏联结是一切的症结所在。

硅谷的精英从学校直通大厂，或者直通一个创始人的身份；他们眼里有一些抽象的关于人的表达：用人成本、员工数量、会员转化、客户获取，但他们在生活中不用面对更多的有血有肉的人。他们从根本上缺乏把脚踩在土地上的实感，或者“基层经验”。而这绝对不仅仅只是硅谷或政府效率部的问题。

现代化带来的个体化现象，加上社交媒体向个人直接的远景投射造成了一种失重现象，一种掌控感的割裂，一种身份上的迷茫。人和社会的边界在哪里？人和世界之间有什么？有人把这种状态描述为“中景缺失”。

我把这个年度总结的副标题取为“拒绝沉没的人”——而当我说拒绝沉没，我真正想说的是重建中景：在宏大叙事和个人经验之外，重新看见他人的具体面目。

但我不是一个怀旧的人。我知道旧的中景也不是什么好东西。

我今年看了一些关于中国乡村的社会学的书，那种中景是很消极的，像有一堵密不透风的墙逼着“人把全部注意力都放到眼前这个自然村里……生活的意义变成了博取村里人的嫉妒、喝彩，缺少改善生活的动力。这个文化里，人际关系的分量太大，把个人挤没了。” [29] 这种中景是病态的，所以中国的城市化在打散传统关系的同时，很快把它扫进了历史的垃圾桶——虽然没有完全扫干净。

关于中景，上一代人面临的问题关于良心和胆量（你敢离开吗，离开之后你能忍住吗？），而我们面临的问题则是希望和诚实。我想，我们越来越能够忍受自己，但越来越不能忍受别人；我们太擅长把一切归为“关我屁事”和“关你屁事”；我们因为一点价值观的龃龉，就转过头去不看那些共享的部分。我们甘于孤独，是我们默认没有哪种共同生活值得花时间去培养和修补。

科技给予了我们拉黑的自由、退群的自由、逃离的自由。但重建需要更多耐心。我不想假装事情与己无关，我不想把洒脱当做一种优雅，我不想说先动情的人就输了。我的想法非常朴素：我需要别人，我也需要别人需要我。


（七）

谁都无法否认，我们处在深刻变动的历史周期里，单是前所未闻的事件就已令人目不暇接；但同样也不难想象，两百年后的《XX简史》里，我们这代人所过的所有生活也只会被概括成寥寥数语。

历史写作需要主旨提炼、高屋建瓴，谁都明白降维总是伴随着细节丢失：在这件事情上，我不会有情绪。只是一旦想到要面临这样的压缩，我就有点喘不上气来。统计学是诡谲的，最大公约数其实什么也不是。那些被平均、被代表的部分，才是真正的生活——是且仅是被抹平的日常波动，才能被称作真正的生活。

谁都无法否认，历史的“相变”时刻总是出现在结构性的失衡的节点上，这其中形势的因素占了很大比重。加州在美国历史上率先给予女性财产权，这并不是立法者良心发现，而是如果不这么做，移民潮带来的男性劳工将越来越成为不安定的因素，而本就极端的男女比例将进一步失衡[30]。废奴宣言来源于林肯道德信念，但更多来源于他的战争手段——解放政策是为了赢得战争，他说：“我最崇高的目标是拯救联邦，而不是要拯救或毁灭奴隶制度。”

但如果我们就此停下追问，认为历史的车轮在自顾自地向前转动，只是偶尔顺手纠正一些不公，随机选择一些幸运观众获得头彩成为英雄，则又掉入了鸟瞰式的陷阱：把一切都归结于机械化的结构，仿佛历史轨迹和人的能动性毫无关系。

一年前我曾在很长一段时间里被吓坏了。我想，如果真的活在历史的垃圾时间里，过一些注定被遗忘的日子，这是件怎样糟糕和扫兴的事情呀！但现在我想通了，历史书中懒得提及的有太多东西，所以历史的大部分本来都是垃圾时间。但这件事有它的积极意义。

这些不被记住的时间里承载的是不明朗的趋势，它代表着生活中滋长出的一些彼此矛盾的新生和苗头，它是多股正在运动的势力，它代表的不是命定的终局，而是“形成”本身。

我今年去过了三个新地方：夏威夷、萨克拉门托、新奥尔良。它们表面上只是一些分散的美国地名，在某本《美国简史》里，它们也许被概括为毫无关联的度假胜地、淘金热起点和爵士故乡。

但我发现这些地名里都藏有的褶皱，而这些褶皱可以被多项式展开。夏威夷是珍珠港袭击的震央，但也有美利坚最纯正花火大会；萨克拉门托用高速公路把城市与河岸隔开，仿佛害怕承认自己曾以水路为生；密西西比河下游的地形像一只鱼骨，那些从脊柱生出来的七百根骨刺状的公路正通往两百年前在此繁殖的七百个种植园，而当这些创伤退潮后，这片沃土依然挣扎，依然是美国最为贫困、空气污染最为严重的地区之一。

我在这些旅途中串珠成线地理解了很多事情：关于国家记忆如何被扭曲和消费，关于资本和开发计划如何碾断或遮掩城市的历史脉络，关于振聋发聩的进步条律如何系统性地受到堵塞，关于经济、阶级、文化如何在不同时间和空间里连续地成为依赖或者桎梏。

是的，在离地三千英尺的天空向下看，我们尽可以作一些决定论式的宣称，比如美洲之所以被殖民是因为它的大陆轴线是竖着长的 [31]。但唯有真正走进街巷，看见剥落的墙体，闻到当地绝无仅有的空气，听到纪念碑和纪念碑之间废语般的杂讯，我们才能受到触动，才能真正理解这些褶皱的尺度。

历史的本质是渐进的。把这些褶皱烫平，它们就会展开成一段看不到尽头的长坡，而不是绊人的台阶——而在任何当下，我们确实处于长坡的起点。


（八）

我想，理解历史，主要是需要理解它的惯性。跨时代的事件不是无端出现，也不会在一朝一夕间改变所有人的生活。它们更像是港口的浮标：水手通过它们来定位，但海面以下，洋流从未停止。

1928年，《平等选举法》通过，英国女性获得投票权。但在此之前的很多日子里，在逐步推进的工业化和城市化中，妇女已经在工厂、医院和办公室里工作。她们越来越多地参与到社会生产关系中来，一点点让权利的适用范围逐渐扩大——法案只是迟到地给这条洋流命名而已。

但纸面的承诺并不代表皆大欢喜。女性的权益不因为拥有投票权而完全改善。同工不同酬、隐形的职业天花板、关于“女强人”和“好女人”的刻板印象……这是些时至今日也未完全解决的问题，而“女性主义”的定义本身，也在当下的舆论中从先锋身份折损为一种性别冲突的叙事。1928年是妇女权益运动一个醒目的节点，在它之前有一条很长的上坡路，在它之后同样也拖着漫长的尾巴。

1965年，《民权法案》废除种族隔离制度，很多发起抵制、静坐的，然后被殴打、被逮捕、被处刑的马丁路德金都没有活到这一天，但如果没有他们在数十年中前赴后继地组织和动员，种族问题从一开始就不会被提上议事日程。

在此之后，明面上的歧视不再被主流所允许，但可疑的街区划分、偏袒的贷款政策、司法量刑的差异仍然继续存在——这些后遗症还需要更多的马丁，而这些马丁可能不再有自己的名字。

所以，当我说“垃圾时间有它的积极意义”时，我并不是在等一个“形成”，然后期待它符合我的胃口。我希望自己采用的一种面向行动的视角：我想写的东西，是在那些尚未沉淀到历史的年份里的故事，因为人究竟在做什么，决定了“相变”时刻最终向哪一边倾斜。

（我想，福柯之所以想要对权力进行积极的解释，而非只是看作一种消极和压制，大概也是出于类似的能动性考虑。）

我这样写下去，是为了做一种细节速写，去对抗纯粹的鸟瞰式的叙事。因为如果只是作为一个记录者，这本身有一种消极的成分在里面——好像我只能躺在河床上，只顾着看粼粼流光从身上漂过去似的 [32]。说到底，我既然身处历史里，就不可能事不关己地宏观起来。

我想始终带以一个普通的人文主义者的视角来进行写作。这些观察或听闻中不可避免有一些值得同情、称赞、扼腕或唾骂的事情，对此我会报以鲜明的态度，但我不想局限在受害者的框架里，不想搞出二元分明的对立，不想讲英雄主义，或塑造些近似神明或恶魔的东西；我不想在故事的最后把一切盖棺在报应、物极必反、否极泰来，或者天道好还上面。

我想，历史总是记住方向和结构，但人是生活在土地上，而不是天空的弧度里。站在地面上，我们看到的是不稳定、不明朗、是善恶混杂的日常；而正是在这样的尺度下，“面向行动”要求我们保持敏感、拥抱触动、以及拒绝习以为常。

我想说自己写作是为了拒绝遗忘，但这又是一种英雄主义的说法。因此我只能说，我思故我在：这个“在”字不是“存在”，而是“应该在哪儿”的意思。
